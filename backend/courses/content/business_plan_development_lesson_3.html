Module 1: Foundations of Business Planning

Lesson 3: Developing a Value Proposition and Setting Business Objectives

Level: Beginner | Suggested duration: 120 minutes

Lesson output: A customer-centred value proposition and a small set of measurable, time-bound objectives.

Lesson purpose

Use what learners observed in Lessons 1 and 2 to explain why a defined customer might choose an offer over current alternatives. Then translate that value into objectives that guide the next learning and execution steps. A value proposition is a hypothesis to test, not a slogan or a claim that the business has already proved.

Learning outcomes

  1. Distinguish customer jobs, pains, and desired gains from product features.
  2. Draft a specific value proposition grounded in a target segment and evidence.
  3. Compare the proposed value with customers' current alternatives.
  4. Write objectives with a measure, baseline, target, owner, and time horizon.
  5. Choose leading and outcome indicators and review them responsibly.

1. From customer problem to value

A value proposition summarizes the customer group, important need or task, benefit offered, and reason the offer may be preferable to alternatives. It connects a customer's situation to a credible outcome. It is not the same as a feature list, mission statement, or advertising tagline.

Separate:

  • Customer job: what a person is trying to accomplish in a functional, social, or emotional context.
  • Pain: an obstacle, risk, cost, frustration, or unwanted outcome encountered before, during, or after that job.
  • Desired gain: an outcome or improvement the person values, including speed, reliability, convenience, confidence, or affordability.
  • Offer: the product, service, or experience the business can actually provide.
  • Evidence: what supports the claim that the need matters and the proposed benefit is valued.

Features matter only insofar as they help a customer make progress. Do not assume that every feature is valuable to every segment. Prioritize the pains and gains customers describe as important, then test whether the offer addresses them.

2. Draft and test a value proposition

Use this working sentence: For [specific customer] who [important situation or need], [offer] helps [measurable or observable improvement] by [how it works]. Unlike [current alternative], it [credible distinction].

Keep the statement concrete. Replace “best quality” with a benefit a customer can recognize; replace “everyone” with the segment supported by current evidence; and replace unsupported superlatives with a testable distinction. A clear proposition makes it easier to select research questions, product priorities, channels, and measures.

Test the proposition by showing a simple description or prototype to people in the target segment. Ask them to explain it in their own words, relate it to a recent experience, identify what is unclear, and compare it with what they do now. Compliments alone are not validation. Record observed actions and limitations as well as stated opinions.

3. Set objectives that guide action

An objective states a meaningful result or learning goal. Good early objectives are specific enough to guide action, measurable with a defined method, achievable with available resources, relevant to the current business risk, and bounded by a review date. The familiar SMART acronym is a checklist, not a substitute for sound judgment.

Distinguish an activity (“conduct interviews”) from an output (“complete 8 interviews with a defined segment”) and an outcome (“identify whether the same problem and workaround recur”). Activities can be counted, but do not automatically prove progress. Pair leading indicators (for example, qualified pilot requests) with outcome measures (for example, completed, paid, repeat use) when appropriate.

For each objective, record:

  • the outcome and why it matters now;
  • the measure, unit, data source, and baseline;
  • the target and deadline, including the reason for the target;
  • the person responsible and resources required;
  • the review date and what decision different results would trigger.

Separate a target from a forecast. A target is something the team intends to achieve; a forecast is its best current estimate of what will happen. Neither should be presented as a verified fact.

Worked example: mobile bicycle repair

Segment hypothesis: weekday bicycle commuters in one service area who recently needed a repair. Evidence to date: a small number of interviewees described difficulty transporting a disabled bicycle to a workshop. The sample does not establish prevalence, willingness to pay, or market size.

Draft proposition: “For weekday bicycle commuters whose bicycle needs repair, a booked mobile repair visit may reduce the time and effort of getting the bicycle to a workshop. We will compare this with the commuter's recent workaround before deciding which service to test.” This is appropriately cautious until a service pilot provides evidence.

Learning objective: “By [date], speak with [a feasible number] of eligible commuters about a recent repair, record their workarounds and consequences, and decide whether transport is important enough to test a mobile visit.” Set the sample and decision rule before collecting responses; the example does not prescribe a universal threshold.

Practical activity

  1. Review the Lesson 2 evidence log. Highlight a repeated customer job, a pain, and a desired gain; mark unsupported beliefs as assumptions.
  2. Draft two versions of the value-proposition sentence for the same segment. Remove claims that cannot yet be supported.
  3. Ask a peer to paraphrase the proposition and name the customer and benefit. Revise any part the peer cannot explain.
  4. Write one customer-learning objective and one delivery objective. For each, state its measure, baseline or current unknown, target, owner, deadline, and next decision.
  5. Identify how the measure could be misleading, and what evidence would challenge the proposition.

Common mistakes

  • Describing a product feature instead of a customer benefit.
  • Combining unrelated customer segments in one proposition.
  • Claiming differentiation without checking alternatives.
  • Setting targets with no baseline, owner, time period, or decision attached.
  • Confusing website traffic or activity counts with customer outcomes.
  • Keeping a target after evidence shows it is unrealistic or no longer relevant.

Knowledge check

  1. Which best describes a value proposition? A. A list of product features. B. A clear statement of customer, need, benefit, and credible distinction. C. A guaranteed sales claim. D. A company name.
  2. What is a useful test of whether a proposition is clear? A. Ask friends if they like it. B. Check whether target customers can explain the offer and connect it to a recent need. C. Add more features. D. Count adjectives.
  3. Which item makes an objective actionable? A. A target with no date. B. A measure, baseline, owner, target, and review date. C. A broad aspiration. D. A competitor slogan.
  4. What is the difference between a target and a forecast? A. They are identical. B. A target is intended; a forecast estimates what is expected to happen. C. A forecast is always guaranteed. D. A target needs no measure.

Answer key: 1-B; 2-B; 3-B; 4-B.

Assignment

Submit a one-page value proposition and objectives sheet. Include the target segment, customer job, strongest evidence and its limitation, proposed benefit, current alternative, two measurable objectives, and one test that could change the proposition. Do not describe assumptions as proven facts.
