Module 2: Market Research and Business Strategy

Lesson 1: Market Research, Target Customers, and Competitor Analysis

Level: Beginner | Suggested duration: 150 minutes

Lesson output: A concise research brief, initial target-segment profile, competitor/alternative comparison, and evidence-based opportunity estimate.

Lesson purpose and learning outcomes

This lesson moves from individual problem discovery to a structured view of the market. Learners combine direct customer research with reliable published information, identify a reachable first segment, compare direct and indirect alternatives, and state what their evidence can and cannot support.

  1. Distinguish primary research from secondary research and match methods to questions.
  2. Describe a target segment using needs, behaviour, role, context, and buying process.
  3. Identify competitors and substitutes, including doing nothing or using an informal workaround.
  4. Use transparent assumptions to estimate a reachable market without presenting unsupported numbers as facts.
  5. Record sources, dates, methods, limitations, and implications for a business decision.

1. Start with a research decision

Research is useful when it reduces uncertainty around a decision. Begin by writing the question and the decision it will inform. Examples: Which customer group should we serve first? How common is the need in this location? What alternatives are customers already paying for? What distribution channel can reach them?

  • Primary research is information collected directly for the question: interviews, observation, surveys, small pilots, or direct conversations with buyers and users.
  • Secondary research is existing information: official statistics, industry reports, published studies, trade data, competitor materials, and relevant local records.

Use interviews and observation to understand context and behaviour. Use reliable datasets and reports for broader patterns. A broad national statistic may not represent a small local customer group; a few interviews cannot establish the size of a whole market.

2. Define the target customer and buying process

Describe the people or organizations that share the relevant need, not simply the largest imaginable audience. Note geography, role, behaviour, use occasion, need intensity, budget or purchasing authority, access, and what event triggers a search for a solution.

In business-to-business settings, separate the user, buyer, decision-maker, approver, and payer; one person may hold more than one role. Identify how customers discover options, evaluate risk, obtain approval, purchase, and use the offer. State who is outside the first research scope and why.

3. Identify competitors and substitutes

A competitor is any option that helps the customer make progress on the same job. Consider direct products, indirect services, DIY approaches, informal providers, existing tools, and the option of postponing the task or doing nothing. Customers compare outcomes, convenience, trust, access, risk, and total cost—not only product features.

Build a comparison with: customer segment served; problem addressed; offer and delivery method; public price or pricing basis; channel and availability; evidence of customer response; strengths; gaps; source and date. Use publicly available information ethically. Do not treat a competitor's marketing claim as independently verified performance.

4. Estimate market opportunity carefully

Market-size labels vary, so define every number and its source. A useful planning sequence is:

  • Total potential market: the broad population or spending category that could conceivably need the offer.
  • Serviceable market: the portion matching the offer, geography, regulations, language, delivery capacity, and customer profile.
  • Reachable initial market: the subset the team can realistically contact and serve during a stated period.

For an estimate, state the number of eligible customers, the period, the purchase frequency, the realistic price or spend, and the evidence behind each assumption. Show the arithmetic. Use low, base, and high cases when uncertainty is material. A reachable initial estimate is not a promise of sales.

5. Evaluate source quality and synthesize

Prefer original, recent, relevant, transparent sources. Record publisher, date, geography, definitions, sample or methodology, and known limitations. Separate observed results from interpretation and forecast. Triangulate a high-impact claim with more than one independent source where feasible. Do not silently fill gaps with invented precision.

Summarize: what appears consistent; what differs by segment; what evidence challenges the opportunity; what remains unknown; and what next test or strategic choice follows.

Worked example: mobile bicycle repair

Define a first research question: “How do weekday bicycle commuters in this service area handle a recent repair need?” Interview recent customers about actual events and current workarounds. Find local counts of bicycle commuters only if an appropriate official or credible dataset exists; record its geography and year. Compare workshops, mobile mechanics, informal repairers, and postponed repairs. Estimate a reachable first market from the service radius, eligible commuters, realistic booking capacity, and transparent repeat-use assumptions. Do not use a global bicycle market figure to claim local demand.

Activity and deliverable

  1. Write one market question and name the decision it will inform.
  2. Define one initial customer segment and a recruitment description.
  3. List at least five alternatives, including indirect or informal options.
  4. Collect three relevant secondary sources. Record publisher, URL, date, geography, and limitation.
  5. Prepare a simple low/base/high reachable-market estimate, showing each input and formula.
  6. End with one decision, one uncertainty, and one next research action.

Knowledge check

  1. Which is primary research? A. An official census table. B. An interview about a recent customer experience. C. An old blog statistic. D. A competitor's slogan.
  2. Why include indirect alternatives? A. Customers only compare identical products. B. Customers may solve the same job through a workaround or by doing nothing. C. It increases market size automatically. D. It proves demand.
  3. What should a market-size estimate include? A. A large number without a source. B. Defined population, period, assumptions, sources, and arithmetic. C. Only a competitor valuation. D. An unqualified forecast.
  4. What can a few interviews establish? A. Exact national market size. B. Guaranteed willingness to pay. C. Directional insight about the people and experiences studied, with stated limits. D. Product-market fit.

Answer key: 1-B; 2-B; 3-B; 4-C.
