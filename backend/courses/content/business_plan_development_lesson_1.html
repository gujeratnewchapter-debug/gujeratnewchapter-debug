Module 1: Foundations of Business Planning

Lesson 1: Introduction to Startup Business Planning

Level: Beginner   Suggested duration: 90 minutes

Lesson output: A one-page initial business idea brief.

Lesson purpose

A business idea is a starting point, not proof that a business opportunity exists. This lesson introduces business planning as a practical process for clarifying an idea, making uncertainty visible, and deciding what to learn or do next. Start with a short working brief rather than trying to write a complete plan before you have evidence.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  1. Define a startup and distinguish it from an established business while recognizing that the categories can overlap.
  2. Explain what a business plan is and how it supports decisions, coordination, communication, and learning.
  3. Describe the differences between lean and traditional business plans.
  4. Identify common sections of a traditional business plan.
  5. Separate evidence from assumptions and testable hypotheses.
  6. Draft an initial business idea brief and identify a practical first test.

Key terms

  • Startup: An organization exploring a product, service, or business model under uncertainty, often seeking a repeatable and sustainable way to serve customers. A startup does not have to be a technology company.
  • Business plan: A structured explanation of what a business intends to do, whom it will serve, how it will operate and earn revenue, and what resources it will need.
  • Lean plan: A concise working summary of key business elements and assumptions, designed to support early learning and frequent updates.
  • Traditional plan: A more detailed business document used when the business or its audience needs a fuller explanation.
  • Fact or evidence: Information supported by a reliable source, record, or direct observation.
  • Assumption: A belief used temporarily to make a decision or estimate, but not yet verified.
  • Hypothesis: A specific statement that can be tested.
  • Experiment: An activity designed to gather evidence, such as interviews, a prototype, a small pilot, or a landing-page test.

1. What is a startup?

A startup is a new or early-stage organization working to discover a business model that can serve customers reliably and sustainably. It usually faces uncertainty about whether the problem matters to customers, who experiences it most strongly, whether the proposed offer solves it well, how the business can reach customers and earn revenue, and whether delivery can cover its costs.

Not every new business is designed for rapid growth. A neighborhood bakery may be a successful small business built to serve a stable local market. A startup may emphasize experimentation and finding a model that can be repeated or expanded. The categories can overlap; the distinction is about the business's aims and stage, not its use of technology.

2. What is a business plan?

A business plan organizes the main decisions behind an opportunity: the customer problem, proposed offer, market, way the business will operate, resources it needs, and how it may become financially sustainable. It turns an idea into decisions and actions that can be examined and revised.

A plan can help a founder:

  • Clarify the customer problem, proposed solution, and expected benefit.
  • Identify what is known and what still needs to be learned.
  • Research customers, alternatives, competitors, and market conditions.
  • Coordinate activities, responsibilities, skills, suppliers, and milestones.
  • Estimate costs, sales assumptions, cash requirements, and funding needs.
  • Explain the opportunity to co-founders, employees, lenders, investors, partners, or advisers.
  • Recognize risks, define responses, and measure progress.

A business plan does not guarantee success or replace customer research. It is a working tool: update it when credible evidence changes an assumption or decision.

3. Lean plan and traditional plan

Lean planTraditional plan
Short, focused working summary.Detailed written explanation.
Useful for early exploration and frequent revision.Useful when a reader needs more detail or formal evaluation.
Often covers the problem, customer, value proposition, solution, channels, revenue, costs, measures, and advantage.Commonly covers the executive summary, company, offer, market, business model, marketing and sales, operations, team, finances, funding request if applicable, and supporting information.
Helps identify and test assumptions.Communicates a fuller case supported by research and forecasts.

The formats are complementary. A founder can start with a lean plan, test important assumptions, and expand it into a traditional plan when the evidence or audience calls for more detail.

4. Common sections of a traditional business plan

  1. Executive summary: A short overview of the business, customer problem, solution, market, team, and key financial needs. It is usually drafted after the rest of the plan.
  2. Company description: What the business does, its purpose, goals, and relevant background.
  3. Product or service: What is offered, how it works, its customer benefit, and its current stage.
  4. Market analysis: Target customers, demand, trends, competitors, alternatives, and supporting evidence.
  5. Business model: How the business creates and delivers value and earns revenue.
  6. Marketing and sales: How customers will discover, evaluate, buy, and continue using the offer.
  7. Operations and management: People, skills, roles, suppliers, processes, equipment, location, and delivery arrangements.
  8. Financial plan: Startup costs, revenue assumptions, operating expenses, and relevant financial statements and analyses.
  9. Funding request, if applicable: The amount required, its intended use, and the proposed financing approach.
  10. Risks and supporting documents: Important risks, mitigation steps, research, permits where relevant, and other appendices.

Not every early-stage idea needs every section in full detail. The plan should fit the business's stage and its intended reader.

5. Separate evidence from uncertainty

Early-stage founders rarely have complete information. Good planning does not hide uncertainty; it labels it and sets out how to learn.

TypeExample
Fact or evidence“In 12 interviews conducted this month, 8 bicycle commuters described difficulty arranging repairs during the workday.”
Assumption“A mobile mechanic can complete four jobs per day in the initial service area.”
Hypothesis“Bicycle commuters will pay a stated fee for a repair visit at their workplace.”
TestInterview target customers about recent repair experiences, then offer a small pilot at a clearly stated price and track bookings.

The interview result is evidence about the people interviewed, not automatic proof of demand across an entire market. Record the source, date, and limitations of evidence. Treat forecasts as estimates based on stated assumptions, not verified outcomes.

6. Guided example: mobile bicycle repair

  • Problem to investigate: A bicycle breakdown is inconvenient, and taking a bicycle to a workshop may take time.
  • Possible customers: Commuters, students, delivery riders, or families who use bicycles.
  • Proposed solution: Customers book a mechanic to meet them at an agreed location.
  • Possible revenue: A fee per repair, maintenance packages, or service agreements with organizations operating bicycle fleets.
  • Important uncertainties: Will customers pay the proposed price? How often will they need a repair? How far can a mechanic travel in a day? Will revenue cover labor, transport, tools, and other costs?
  • Small first test: Speak with a selected group of bicycle users about their most recent repair experience. If interest is credible, run a limited pilot with clear pricing and measure inquiries, bookings, completion time, feedback, and cost.

This example illustrates planning questions; it is not evidence that the business will succeed. Test demand, costs, pricing, and operational capacity before making strong claims.

7. Beginner's planning questions

  1. What specific problem am I trying to solve?
  2. Who experiences this problem most strongly?
  3. How do those people solve it today?
  4. What might my solution do better, faster, more conveniently, or more affordably?
  5. What evidence suggests that customers care about this problem?
  6. How might the business earn revenue?
  7. What resources and skills will I need to test the idea?
  8. What are the three most important assumptions to validate?
  9. What is the smallest affordable experiment I can run next?

8. Common early planning mistakes

  • Starting with a product idea without identifying a customer problem.
  • Defining everyone as a potential customer instead of selecting a specific group.
  • Claiming a market is large without showing relevant evidence.
  • Copying competitors without explaining a meaningful customer benefit.
  • Forecasting sales based on optimism rather than transparent assumptions.
  • Ignoring cash timing: a business can appear profitable on paper and still lack cash to pay its bills.
  • Treating the plan as fixed instead of revising it when new evidence emerges.
  • Reusing figures or templates without checking that they fit the business and its local context.

Practical assignment: one-page initial business idea brief

Task: Write a one-page initial brief for a business idea. This is a starting document, not the complete business plan. Label anything uncertain as an assumption or hypothesis.

  1. Working business name.
  2. Customer problem, in two or three sentences.
  3. Specific target customer group.
  4. Proposed product or service.
  5. Why customers might choose it over current alternatives.
  6. Possible way to earn revenue.
  7. Three assumptions that need testing.
  8. One low-cost experiment to test the most important assumption. State what you will do and what evidence you will record.

Assessment rubric (10 points)

CriterionPoints
Customer problem is clear and specific.0–2
Proposed solution is understandable.0–2
Customer benefit and target group are plausible.0–2
Revenue idea is identified and understandable.0–2
Assumptions are testable and the proposed experiment is practical.0–2

A score of 8/10 indicates a strong initial understanding. The idea still needs evidence before its assumptions should be treated as facts.

Knowledge check

  1. What is a business plan primarily used for? A. Guaranteeing success. B. Organizing and testing business decisions. C. Avoiding market research. D. Replacing customer feedback.
  2. Which statement best describes a lean plan? A. A full legal contract. B. A concise working summary of key business assumptions. C. A tax return. D. A guarantee of funding.
  3. Which is an assumption rather than verified evidence? A. A dated invoice was paid. B. A survey has 50 recorded responses. C. The business will get 100 customers in month one. D. A prototype was tested by five users.
  4. Why should a founder update a business plan? A. Plans must never change. B. New evidence may change assumptions and decisions. C. Readers dislike evidence. D. To make the plan longer.
  5. What is a useful first step before investing heavily? A. Rent the largest available office. B. Hire a large team immediately. C. Test the riskiest assumption with a small experiment. D. Forecast unlimited growth.

Answer key: 1-B, 2-B, 3-C, 4-B, 5-C.
