Module 3: Financial Planning and the Complete Business Plan

Lesson 1: Startup Costs, Funding Requirements, and Financial Assumptions

Level: Beginner | Suggested duration: 135 minutes

Lesson output: A categorized startup-cost schedule, initial cash requirement estimate, assumptions log, and funding-option comparison.

Lesson purpose and learning outcomes

Before opening or scaling, founders need to understand what must be paid, when cash is needed, and which uncertainties affect the estimate. This lesson separates one-time launch costs from ongoing costs, distinguishes cash needs from accounting profit, and compares funding sources without assuming that outside investment is always appropriate.

  1. Identify pre-launch and recurring cost categories relevant to a chosen venture.
  2. Distinguish fixed, variable, one-time, recurring, and working-capital needs.
  3. Build a cash requirement estimate with dates, sources, and explicit assumptions.
  4. Compare funding options by cost, control, repayment, risk, availability, and fit.
  5. Use scenarios and a contingency rather than false precision.

1. Build a complete cost inventory

List costs required to prepare, launch, and operate through the period before reliable incoming cash. Relevant items may include registration and permits; research and professional services; equipment and setup; technology; inventory and supplies; location and utilities; wages or contractor costs; insurance; marketing; delivery; payment and banking fees; tax obligations; loan payments; and contingency for plausible overruns.

Classify each item as:

  • One-time or setup: incurred to prepare the business or acquire a long-lived asset.
  • Recurring: expected on a regular schedule, such as rent, software, or salaries.
  • Fixed or variable: relatively stable within a relevant operating range, or changing with output/transactions.
  • Working capital: cash needed to cover timing gaps between paying obligations and collecting from customers.

Categories can overlap; note how each behaves. A deposit may require cash even if it is not an expense in the income statement. Equipment may be an asset and later depreciated rather than fully expensed immediately. Confirm accounting treatment with a qualified local professional.

2. Estimate the amount and timing

For each cost, record quantity, unit price, source or quote date, payment timing, tax/currency treatment, and whether the number is confirmed or estimated. Separate a supplier quote from a founder guess. Include a modest, explained contingency for uncertainty rather than padding every line without rationale.

A basic funding need estimate is: planned pre-launch spending + expected operating cash deficit during the launch period + working-capital buffer + contingency − available founder funds and confirmed receipts. Build this by month because a business may need cash before revenue arrives. State the period covered and assumptions about customer payment delays, inventory, seasonality, and deposits.

Prepare at least a base case and a downside case for uncertain volume, price, timing, or costs. Identify the trigger that would make the founder delay hiring, reduce scope, seek additional funds, or stop spending.

3. Compare funding options

Possible sources include founder savings, retained earnings, grants, customer prepayments or deposits, supplier credit, loans, crowdfunding, angel or venture investment, and strategic partnerships. Each has different availability and obligations. Compare cost of capital, repayment schedule, collateral or guarantees, ownership dilution, control, eligibility, time to obtain funds, restrictions, and downside risk.

Debt creates repayment obligations whether the forecast succeeds or not. Equity exchanges ownership and may involve governance rights. Grants can be restricted and competitive. Customer prepayments require clear terms and a credible delivery plan. Do not treat an application, investor conversation, or unsigned commitment as secured funding.

Funding terms, tax, securities, consumer, and lending rules vary by country and business type. Use qualified local financial and legal advice before accepting regulated investments, taking deposits, or signing financing agreements.

Worked example: mobile bicycle repair

Potential setup costs: tools, safety equipment, initial parts stock, booking/payment setup, and permits if required. Ongoing costs: mechanic time, travel, replacement parts, phone/data, insurance, payment fees, and marketing. Working-capital risk: parts may need to be purchased before a customer pays; travel and labor still cost money if a booking cancels. Obtain dated supplier quotes, estimate service capacity, and model a slow-start case. A prepayment pilot is appropriate only with transparent terms and the ability to deliver or refund.

Activity and deliverable

  1. Make a cost register with category, quantity, estimate, source/date, timing, frequency, and confidence.
  2. Separate setup spending, monthly operating costs, and working capital.
  3. Prepare base and downside monthly cash-need estimates for an explicitly stated launch period.
  4. Compare three realistic funding sources using cost, control, repayment, eligibility, time, and risk.
  5. Write the next action that would improve the least reliable, highest-impact estimate.

Knowledge check

  1. Why can a profitable business still need funding? A. Profit equals cash exactly. B. Payments and collections occur at different times, creating working-capital needs. C. Costs never recur. D. Revenue is always paid in advance.
  2. What should support a cost estimate? A. An unlabeled guess. B. Quantity, unit, source/date, timing, and confidence. C. A competitor's revenue. D. A fixed percentage with no reason.
  3. What is a key debt risk? A. No repayment. B. Repayment is due even if results are below plan. C. It never has a cost. D. It transfers ownership automatically.
  4. When is funding confirmed? A. After an introductory conversation. B. When a founder plans to apply. C. When the funding is formally committed and conditions are understood. D. When it appears in a forecast.

Answer key: 1-B; 2-B; 3-B; 4-C.
