Module 3: Financial Planning and the Complete Business Plan

Lesson 2: Financial Projections: Income Statement, Cash Flow, Balance Sheet, and Break-Even Analysis

Level: Beginner | Suggested duration: 165 minutes

Lesson output: A simple, assumption-led forecast with an income statement, cash-flow schedule, opening and forecast balance sheet, and break-even estimate.

Lesson purpose and learning outcomes

Financial projections translate the business model into a set of connected estimates. They are not promises. Learners practise the difference between profit and cash, link statements, document assumptions, and use break-even as a planning tool while recognizing its limits.

  1. Explain what the income statement, cash-flow statement, and balance sheet each show.
  2. Build a basic sales and expense forecast using transparent operational assumptions.
  3. Recognize why accounting profit differs from cash movement.
  4. Describe assets, liabilities, and equity and the balance-sheet equation.
  5. Estimate break-even units or revenue under stated assumptions.
  6. Use scenarios and sensitivity checks to identify financial risks and decisions.

1. Begin with operating assumptions

Forecasts should follow the business model. Write assumptions for customer volume, conversion, price, frequency, refunds, capacity, collection time, variable costs, payroll, rent, taxes, inventory, equipment, and financing. Tie sales to a customer or operational driver rather than applying an unexplained growth percentage.

For each assumption, record source, date, confidence, owner, and a way to test it. Separate observed results from targets and forecasts. Use monthly periods during launch so cash timing and seasonality are visible. Avoid precision that the evidence does not justify.

2. Income statement: performance over a period

The income statement summarizes revenue, expenses, and resulting profit or loss over a defined period. A simplified structure is: revenue − cost of goods/services sold = gross profit; gross profit − operating expenses = operating result; then account for financing costs, taxes, and other items to estimate net profit or loss. Classification depends on the accounting framework and business; use an accountant for formal statements.

Revenue is usually recognized according to applicable accounting rules, which may differ from the date cash is received. Equipment purchases, depreciation, inventory accounting, tax, and debt all affect how a simple teaching example translates into formal accounts.

3. Cash flow: when money moves

A cash-flow schedule records cash received and cash paid during each period. A simple forecast begins with opening cash, adds expected collections and financing, subtracts operating payments, asset purchases, debt payments, and taxes, and calculates closing cash. Closing cash becomes the next period's opening cash.

Cash can be tight even when a forecast shows profit: customers may pay late, inventory may be bought early, equipment requires upfront payment, or debt principal must be repaid. Conversely, a loan can increase cash but is not sales revenue. Track minimum cash and the date a shortfall could occur.

4. Balance sheet: position at a point in time

The balance sheet describes resources and claims at a specific date. The core equation is Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Assets may include cash, receivables, inventory, and equipment; liabilities may include supplier payables, loans, and taxes due; equity represents owners' residual interest, subject to the applicable accounting rules.

The balance sheet is a snapshot, while the income statement and cash-flow statement cover periods. Profit is not the same as cash or equity. Owner contributions, borrowing, asset purchases, depreciation, customer collections, and retained profit affect statements in different ways.

5. Break-even analysis

For a single product or service, a simple break-even calculation is: contribution per unit = selling price per unit − variable cost per unit; break-even units = fixed costs for the period ÷ contribution per unit. Break-even revenue is break-even units multiplied by selling price, under the same simplifying assumptions.

This estimate assumes a stable price, cost, product mix, and capacity and typically ignores timing, tax, financing, and step changes in cost. For several products, a weighted contribution margin or more detailed model may be needed. A break-even point is not a guarantee that sales volume is achievable or cash will be available.

Worked example

Suppose a repair service charges 50 currency units per visit, with 20 in variable labor, parts, and travel for each visit, and fixed monthly costs of 600. Contribution is 30 per visit; simple operating break-even is 20 visits for that month (600 ÷ 30), assuming all visits have the same price and cost. The example excludes taxes, financing, owner compensation if not included in fixed cost, seasonality, cancellations, and collection delays. Learners must replace the values with sourced local estimates.

Build and connect the projections

  1. Estimate monthly units and price; calculate revenue.
  2. Estimate variable costs per unit and fixed operating expenses; calculate an income statement.
  3. Translate accounting items into actual payment and collection timing for the cash-flow schedule.
  4. Record cash, receivables, inventory, equipment, payables, debt, and equity on opening and forecast balance sheets.
  5. Check the balance-sheet equation and reconcile closing cash to the cash-flow schedule.
  6. Calculate break-even using the same period and cost definitions; compare with capacity and reachable demand.
  7. Create base and downside cases and identify which assumption changes the decision most.

Common errors

  • Calling cash received from a loan “sales.”
  • Confusing profit with cash in the bank.
  • Forecasting volume beyond delivery capacity.
  • Leaving out taxes, fees, owner compensation, maintenance, refunds, or payment delays.
  • Using a break-even formula without defining period, unit, variable cost, and fixed costs.
  • Presenting a single forecast with no assumptions, sensitivity, or downside case.

Knowledge check

  1. Which statement reports financial position at a date? A. Income statement. B. Balance sheet. C. Marketing plan. D. Sales pipeline.
  2. Why can profit differ from cash? A. Timing and non-cash accounting items. B. They are always identical. C. Loans are sales. D. Cash flow excludes payments.
  3. In a simple break-even formula, contribution per unit is: A. Price minus variable cost per unit. B. Fixed cost plus tax. C. Cash minus debt. D. Total revenue divided by customers.
  4. What does borrowing do? A. It is sales revenue. B. It can increase cash and creates a liability/repayment obligation. C. It guarantees profit. D. It removes operating costs.

Answer key: 1-B; 2-A; 3-A; 4-B.
