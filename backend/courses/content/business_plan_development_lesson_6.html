Module 2: Market Research and Business Strategy

Lesson 3: Marketing, Sales, Operations, and Team Planning

Level: Beginner | Suggested duration: 150 minutes

Lesson output: A customer journey, first-channel and sales plan, delivery workflow, and role/responsibility map.

Lesson purpose and learning outcomes

This lesson connects the business promise to how customers discover and purchase the offer and how the team delivers it. Marketing, sales, operations, and team responsibilities should reinforce the same value proposition. Learners create a realistic first operating plan rather than assuming growth happens automatically.

  1. Map a customer's path from awareness through purchase, delivery, support, and retention.
  2. Choose marketing channels and measures that fit a defined segment and budget.
  3. Describe a repeatable, customer-centred sales process.
  4. Map the operational steps, resources, suppliers, quality checks, and risks required for delivery.
  5. Assign clear responsibilities and identify capability gaps.

1. Marketing: reach the right customers

Marketing is the system for understanding and reaching the intended customer, explaining relevant value, and supporting informed choice. It includes positioning, message, channel, offer, budget, timing, and measurement. A channel is useful when the target customers use it and the business can serve the demand it creates.

Choose a small number of channels to test. Possible channels include direct outreach, referrals, community or trade organizations, retail partners, search, social media, events, or content. State the audience, message, call to action, cost, owner, and tracking method. Use truthful claims, respect privacy and consent, and comply with applicable advertising and consumer-protection requirements.

Track the customer journey with fit-for-purpose measures: qualified reach, inquiries, appointments, conversion, acquisition cost, time to close, activation, repeat use, referral, or complaint rate. Reach and clicks are not sales; sales do not automatically mean profitable or retained customers.

2. Sales: guide a real buying decision

A sales process identifies a qualified need, explains the offer and its limits, answers questions, agrees terms, completes the purchase, and follows up. Document who buys, what evidence they need, common objections, approval steps, expected time, and next action. In business-to-business sales, include procurement and payment cycles.

Use active listening and accurate records; do not promise outcomes the business cannot control. Track conversion by stage so the team can locate friction rather than treating every lost sale as a marketing problem.

3. Operations: deliver the promise reliably

Map the workflow from customer request to fulfilment and follow-up. Identify each step, responsible person, estimated time, quality standard, required input, handoff, and failure point. Include capacity, scheduling, customer support, returns or rework, data handling, health and safety, and contingency arrangements when relevant.

Capacity is a constraint: estimate the number of service units the team can deliver with available hours, equipment, stock, and travel. Do not forecast sales above credible delivery capacity without a plan to add resources. Set a few quality measures such as on-time delivery, error/rework rate, completion time, or customer-reported outcome.

4. Team and responsibility planning

List the capabilities essential to the next milestone, who currently provides them, and gaps that require training, hiring, contracting, partnership, or a simpler first offer. Separate job titles from responsibilities: early ventures may have one person carrying several roles. Make accountability explicit with a responsible owner, decision authority, and deadline.

Consider legal obligations and local rules related to employment, contractors, insurance, safety, data, licensing, and taxation. Requirements vary by jurisdiction; use qualified local guidance for decisions with legal or regulatory consequences.

Worked example: mobile bicycle repair

A first test might reach commuters through one workplace or cycling group rather than broad paid advertising. The sales flow could be: receive inquiry, collect only necessary bicycle and location details, confirm repair scope and transparent estimate, book a time, complete agreed work, record parts and time, collect payment, and ask for permission to follow up. Operations must account for travel between visits, mechanic capacity, parts availability, safety, cancellations, and work that requires a workshop. A partnership with a fleet operator could be tested separately from individual bookings.

Activity and deliverable

  1. Sketch the customer journey and identify one point where trust or clarity is essential.
  2. Select one initial channel and define audience, message, call to action, cost limit, owner, and measure.
  3. Map the order-to-delivery workflow and mark capacity constraints and failure points.
  4. Create a simple responsibility table for the next 30 days: task, owner, decision authority, deadline, and needed support.
  5. List one marketing risk, one operational risk, and a practical response to each.

Knowledge check

  1. What should guide channel choice? A. A trend alone. B. Customer behaviour, fit, delivery capacity, and measurable cost. C. The number of platforms available. D. The largest possible audience.
  2. Why map sales stages? A. To guarantee every sale. B. To locate friction and identify useful next actions. C. To avoid customer questions. D. To replace operations.
  3. Why compare forecast demand with delivery capacity? A. Marketing is unnecessary. B. The business must be able to fulfil the promise reliably. C. Capacity always equals sales. D. Costs do not depend on volume.
  4. What should a small team plan identify? A. Only job titles. B. Responsibilities, owners, decision authority, and capability gaps. C. Every future hire. D. A fixed structure that never changes.

Answer key: 1-B; 2-B; 3-B; 4-B.
