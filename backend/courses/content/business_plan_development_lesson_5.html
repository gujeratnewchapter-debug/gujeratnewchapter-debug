Module 2: Market Research and Business Strategy

Lesson 2: Business Models, Revenue Streams, and Pricing

Level: Beginner | Suggested duration: 135 minutes

Lesson output: A one-page business-model map, revenue assumptions, and a pricing test plan.

Lesson purpose and learning outcomes

A business model explains how an organization creates value for a customer, delivers it, and captures enough value to continue operating. A strong product idea is not yet a viable model. Learners connect their value proposition to customers, channels, revenue, activities, partners, and costs, then test pricing assumptions.

  1. Explain the difference between a value proposition and a business model.
  2. Map a customer segment, problem, offer, channel, relationship, revenue, resources, activities, partners, and costs.
  3. Compare common revenue models and identify who pays, for what, and when.
  4. Distinguish revenue, contribution, gross margin, and profit at a basic level.
  5. Design an ethical pricing test and state its limits.

1. Map the business model

Use a one-page canvas as a set of connected hypotheses rather than a validated blueprint. Ask:

  • Customers: Who uses the offer, who decides, and who pays?
  • Value: What customer job or pain does the offer address?
  • Channels and relationships: How will customers discover, buy, receive, and get support for it?
  • Revenue: What is sold, at what unit, by whom, how often, and on what payment terms?
  • Activities, resources, partners: What must be done or obtained to deliver the promise?
  • Costs: Which costs are fixed, variable, one-time, recurring, or capacity-dependent?

Check consistency. A low-price offer that needs expensive personal service may not cover its delivery cost. A subscription requires recurring value and retention, not just a recurring charge. A marketplace must serve multiple sides and manage the costs of trust, matching, and liquidity.

2. Choose and test a revenue model

Revenue models include direct sales, service fees, subscriptions, usage-based fees, licensing, commissions, leases, advertising, or combinations. These are options, not guarantees. Evaluate the customer's purchasing habits, predictability of value, cost to serve, transaction frequency, payment infrastructure, local laws, and cash timing.

Write each revenue stream as: payer + offer + unit + price or pricing rule + frequency + payment timing. For example: “The rider pays a quoted fee per completed repair, payable when the service is completed.” A possible business-to-business stream might be a monthly fleet-maintenance agreement. Verify who has budget authority and what service level is expected.

3. Set a pricing approach

Pricing decisions should consider customer value and alternatives, costs and required margin, and the strategic position of the offer. Cost-plus pricing can help establish a floor but does not tell you what customers value. Competitor prices are context, not proof that your costs or customers are identical. Willingness-to-pay questions are directional; real choices under clear terms provide stronger evidence, but still have limits.

Possible approaches include a fixed fee, tiered packages, subscription, usage-based rate, bundle, or quote based on scope. State inclusions, exclusions, cancellation/refund terms, taxes or additional charges, and what happens if the service cannot be delivered. Use transparent terms; never disguise a test as a real service or take payment without authority to fulfil the offer.

4. Understand basic unit economics

Revenue is the value earned from sales. Variable costs change with units or transactions; fixed costs remain broadly stable over a relevant range and period. Contribution per unit is selling price minus variable cost per unit. Contribution helps pay fixed costs; it is not the same as net profit or cash in the bank.

For a simple single-product example, break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit, assuming the unit price, cost structure, and mix remain stable. The formula is a planning approximation; multiple products, taxes, capacity constraints, financing costs, and changing prices require more careful analysis.

Worked example: mobile bicycle repair

Possible models include a fee per completed visit, a prepaid maintenance package, or a contract with an organization that operates bicycles. For each, estimate mechanic time, travel, parts, payment fees, cancellations, and customer support. Compare what the commuter currently spends in money and time, but do not treat that estimate as accepted willingness to pay. Pilot one clearly defined service and track qualified requests, completed repairs, travel time, parts cost, contribution, repeat requests, and complaints.

Activity and deliverable

  1. Map the nine connected business-model questions in one page.
  2. Write two alternative revenue streams and identify payer, unit, timing, and delivery obligation for each.
  3. List fixed and variable costs for one sale or service visit.
  4. Compare two pricing approaches using customer value, competitor context, cost to serve, and strategic goal.
  5. Design one transparent pricing test with a pre-set measure and a stop/change decision.

Knowledge check

  1. What does a business model describe? A. Only the product. B. How value is created, delivered, and captured. C. Only the logo. D. A guaranteed profit.
  2. Why is cost-plus pricing not sufficient alone? A. Costs never matter. B. It does not establish customer value or willingness to choose the offer. C. It determines demand exactly. D. It replaces competitor research.
  3. What is contribution per unit in a simple model? A. Price minus variable cost per unit. B. Total assets minus liabilities. C. Revenue minus every expense. D. Cash balance.
  4. What is needed for a subscription model? A. A recurring charge only. B. Ongoing customer value and workable retention and service economics. C. No customer support. D. Guaranteed annual growth.

Answer key: 1-B; 2-B; 3-A; 4-B.
