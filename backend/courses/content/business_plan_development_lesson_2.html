Module 1: Foundations of Business Planning

Lesson 2: Identifying Customer Problems and Testing Business Ideas

Business Plan Development course | Detailed facilitator and learner guide

Discover | Record | Test | Learn

Lesson purpose

This lesson moves learners from an idea they find interesting to an evidence-based understanding of a customer problem. Learners practise defining a problem in the customer's context, choosing an initial customer group, conducting respectful discovery conversations, organizing evidence, and designing a small test that informs a real decision.

The goal is not to prove that every idea is good. It is to learn whether a problem matters to a specific group, how people handle it today, and what uncertainty should be tested next. A thoughtful result may support the idea, narrow it, change it, or show that it should be stopped.

Guiding question: What have customers actually experienced, what do they do today, and what is the smallest ethical test that could change the next business decision?

Position in the course

Lesson 1 introduced startup planning and the distinction between facts, assumptions, hypotheses, and tests. Lesson 2 applies that distinction to customer problems. Lesson 3 will use the strongest early evidence to develop a value proposition and set objectives. Broader market estimates, location, saturation, and competitor prices require additional research in Module 2.

Previous learner artifact: One-page initial business idea brief.

Lesson 2 learner artifacts: Solution-neutral customer problem statement, evidence log, and experiment card.

Next step: Bring the problem statement and evidence into Lesson 3. The value proposition should follow from customer evidence, not replace it.

Lesson at a glance

  • Suggested duration: 150 minutes; may be divided into two 75-minute sessions.
  • Audience: Beginner founders or learners developing an early venture idea.
  • Prerequisite: Lesson 1, or a basic understanding of a business idea and customer.
  • Core output: A customer problem statement, defined initial segment, short evidence synthesis, and one prioritized experiment.
  • Materials: Paper or shared document, sticky notes, pens, timer, board or screen, Lesson 1 idea brief, interview worksheet, and experiment card.
  • Technology: None required. A spreadsheet or shared board is optional.
  • Assessment: Interview practice, completed evidence log, experiment card, knowledge check, and follow-up discovery assignment.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, learners can:

  1. Explain the difference between a customer problem, a proposed solution, and a business idea.
  2. Describe an initial customer segment using relevant behaviours, role, context, or triggering situation.
  3. Write a solution-neutral problem statement naming the customer, situation, obstacle, consequence, and current workaround.
  4. Ask open questions about recent behaviour without pitching or leading the participant.
  5. Record evidence separately from interpretation and assumptions.
  6. Select a high-risk, decision-relevant uncertainty and design a low-cost test with an observable measure and decision rule.
  7. Gather early evidence with voluntary participation, clear purpose, and appropriate privacy care.

Facilitator preparation

Before class:

  • Ask learners to bring their Lesson 1 idea brief. If they do not have one, provide a simple idea prompt or use the shared bicycle-repair example.
  • Prepare a vague or solution-led problem statement and a specific, solution-neutral example.
  • Prepare a role-play prompt with a real or fictional recent experience. Do not show the full scenario to the interviewer in advance.
  • Decide how learners will record notes without collecting unnecessary personal information.
  • For in-person sessions, arrange pairs or groups of three. For online sessions, prepare breakout pairs and a shared worksheet.
  • Explain that learners should not contact vulnerable people, collect sensitive information, promise a service that does not exist, or make a paid offer without clear terms.

Facilitator board headings: Customer, Situation, Task, Obstacle, Consequence, Workaround, Evidence, and Open Question. Prepare copies of the interview guide, evidence log, and experiment card.

Key concepts and teaching notes

1. Separate the problem from the solution

A business idea often starts with a proposed product: an app, shop, service, or device. A customer problem describes the situation or task that makes an outcome difficult. Starting with the solution can bias interviews: the founder may ask questions that invite agreement, and the customer may respond politely without having the problem.

Solution-led statement: “People need our booking app.” This assumes both that people have a meaningful problem and that an app is the right answer.

Problem-led statement: “Independent tutors spend time coordinating schedule changes across separate messages, and late changes can leave lesson time unused.” This is still a hypothesis until investigated, but it describes a context, a difficulty, and a possible consequence.

A useful problem description includes:

  • Customer: Who experiences, pays for, decides about, or is affected by the situation?
  • Task or desired progress: What is the person trying to do?
  • Context or trigger: When and where does the difficulty happen?
  • Obstacle: What makes progress difficult?
  • Consequence: What does the difficulty cost in time, money, effort, risk, or opportunity?
  • Current response: What workaround, alternative, or non-action does the person use?

Problem statement pattern: “[Specific customer group] has difficulty [making progress on a task] when [context or trigger], because [obstacle]. This leads to [consequence]. They currently [workaround or alternative].”

This pattern is a thinking aid. Learners may not yet know every answer. Mark unknown elements as questions instead of filling them with guesses.

2. Define an initial customer segment

“Everyone” is too broad for early discovery. A useful initial segment is a group of people who share a relevant role, context, behaviour, need, or triggering event. It should be narrow enough to recruit and compare experiences. Plan recruitment before interviewing and account for relevant perspectives and community dynamics.

Potential segment clues include role (buyer, user, worker, decision-maker, caregiver, owner, or affected person); context (industry, neighbourhood, work setting, school, household, transport mode, or service environment); behaviour (a task done regularly, product already used, or repeated workaround); trigger (breakdown, deadline, new responsibility, or seasonal change); need intensity (frequency, consequences, time or money spent, urgency); and whether the founder can reach participants respectfully and safely.

A segment is an initial learning focus, not necessarily a final market definition. State who is included, who is not included yet, and why the distinction helps learning.

Example: Instead of “people who ride bicycles,” try “weekday bicycle commuters in one service area who experienced a repair need in the last three months.” This remains a hypothesis, but makes recruitment and comparison more focused.

3. Learn from real behaviour

People can be encouraging because they like the founder, want to be helpful, or imagine a future that never arrives. “I would use that” or “That sounds great” is weak evidence by itself. Ask first about a specific past event and how the person responded.

A useful discovery conversation moves through four stages: Context (understand the person's role and situation); Recent event (ask about the last time the problem occurred); Response (learn what the person did, tried, paid, delayed, or avoided); and Meaning (understand the consequence, frequency, and why the issue mattered).

Good question: “Tell me about the last time you needed to get a bicycle repaired. What happened from the moment you noticed the problem?”

Useful follow-ups: “What did you do next?” “How long did that take?” “What alternatives did you consider?” “What was the hardest part?”

Leading question: “Wouldn’t it be easier if a mechanic came to you?”

Premature sales question: “Would you buy our mobile repair service for this price?”

The last two questions should not replace discovery questions. Use them only after understanding the current experience and when deliberately testing a clear offer. Ask one question at a time. Use neutral language. Do not argue, explain away a concern, or reward answers that support the founder's idea. Silence can give a participant time to remember details.

4. Use multiple evidence sources appropriately

Early evidence may come from interviews about recent experiences; direct observation of a task or workaround, with permission; existing records such as receipts, service logs, wait times, complaints, or appointment data when lawfully and appropriately available; small tests of a prototype, sample service, or clearly described offer; and reliable published research for broader context, with source and date recorded.

Each source answers different questions. Interviews can reveal context and meaning. Observation can expose work people may not remember to mention. Records can help estimate frequency or cost. A prototype test can show where a process is confusing. Published data can provide market context but may not describe the learner's exact customer or location.

Strengthen evidence quality by recording the date, source, number and type of participants or records, method, what was observed or reported, and what the method cannot establish. Capture participants' words rather than replacing them with the founder's interpretation.

Be cautious when interpreting one unusually vivid story; friends or family who want to encourage the founder; social-media likes, page views, or survey clicks without a clear customer action; stated willingness to pay without a real offer or choice; large market numbers not tied to the selected group; or a small convenience sample presented as representative of everyone.

Small samples are useful for learning and improving a test. Interviews are early, directional evidence; a real customer action can provide a stronger signal, but neither should be described beyond what the method shows. Small samples do not establish the size of the whole market or guarantee demand.

5. Distinguish evidence, interpretation, assumption, and hypothesis

  • Evidence: A source-based observation, record, or participant report. Example: “Three interviewed riders said they postponed a repair because transporting the bicycle was difficult.”
  • Interpretation: What the founder thinks the evidence may mean. Example: “Transport may be an important barrier for some riders.”
  • Assumption: A belief used for planning without enough evidence yet. Example: “A mechanic can complete six jobs in a day.”
  • Hypothesis: A testable statement predicting an observable result. Example: “Among weekday commuters who recently needed a repair, a defined share will rank transport to a workshop among their two biggest difficulties.”

Do not change participants' words to make a pattern sound stronger. Separate quotations or faithful notes from summaries, and keep contradictory evidence visible.

6. Choose a test that can change a decision

A useful experiment answers a question the founder must resolve before spending more time or money. State a testable, precise, discrete hypothesis and rank it by risk and importance before choosing the method. Start with the smallest practical test that can inform a decision.

  • Problem interviews: Are the situation and consequence real for the chosen segment?
  • Observation: What steps and workarounds actually occur?
  • Paper sketch or demonstration: Can people understand the proposed process?
  • Manual or concierge pilot: Can the team deliver the service and learn what customers request?
  • Transparent interest page or sign-up: Does a clearly stated offer prompt a meaningful action?
  • Small paid pilot or preorder: Will an appropriate customer make a real commitment under clear terms?

A test is not useful merely because it is easy to run. It must connect the question to the method and to a decision. Choose the method for the evidence it can produce. If a test can only produce compliments, it may not answer whether the problem is important or whether a customer will act.

Experiment card: “We believe [customer group] experiences [problem] in [context]. We will [method] with [participants or records] by [date]. We will consider the result promising if [observable threshold]. If it is not promising, we will [specific next decision].”

Set the success threshold before the test, not after seeing results. A threshold is not proof; it is a pre-agreed rule for deciding what to learn next.

7. Conduct discovery respectfully

  • Explain who you are, why you are asking, and how notes will be used. Participation should be voluntary.
  • Ask permission before recording audio, video, photographs, screenshots, or identifying details.
  • Collect only what is needed. Avoid sensitive personal, financial, health, or identity information unless essential and handled appropriately.
  • Remove names from class notes when they are not needed, and store notes to prevent casual access.
  • Be transparent when an offer is only a concept. Do not imply a service is ready, promise benefits, or accept money without clear terms.
  • Respect language, accessibility, culture, time, and power differences. Do not pressure people or make participation a condition for receiving essential services.

Worked example: mobile bicycle-repair idea

This example continues the business-planning scenario from Lesson 1. A founder is considering a mobile bicycle-repair service.

  1. List what is known and unknown. Evidence might include personally seeing a rider walk a bicycle to a workshop, a service provider's dated repair-request record, or a participant's description of a recent repair. Unknowns include how often this occurs in the chosen area, which riders experience it most, whether transport is the main obstacle, whether riders will pay for a mobile visit, and whether the service can cover costs.
  2. Narrow the learning group. Initial segment hypothesis: weekday bicycle commuters in one reachable area who had a repair need in the past three months. Other groups may be compared later, but should not be mixed into the first small test without a reason.
  3. Draft a solution-neutral problem statement. “Bicycle commuters in the selected area who experience a weekday breakdown may struggle to get a disabled bicycle to a workshop, causing missed trips or time spent arranging transport. Some ask a friend, use a nearby workshop, postpone the repair, or make a temporary fix.” This contains hypotheses; it does not show that mobile repair is the best solution.
  4. Prepare a discovery interview. Ask about the most recent repair, what the commuter tried, how the bicycle reached the repairer, what was difficult, what it cost, and what happened if repair was delayed. After that story, ask whether transport was among the most important difficulties.
  5. Select a first experiment. Hypothesis: “Among weekday bicycle commuters in the selected area who needed a repair in the last three months, a meaningful share experienced transport to a workshop as one of their two most important difficulties.” Interview people meeting the criteria and record recent behaviour and workarounds.

Decision rule: If the expected pattern is uncommon, interview another segment or revise the problem statement before testing a mobile service. If it appears repeatedly, plan a separate service and payment test.

Limit: This early test may guide the next step, but does not establish market size, monthly demand, price acceptance, retention, or full operating costs. Select sample size and thresholds for the learner's context and capacity; no example threshold is a universal standard.

Detailed 150-minute facilitation plan

  • 0–10 | Opening and recall. Retrieve the Lesson 1 brief. Underline the customer, problem, and biggest untested belief. Invite two volunteers to share; ask what customer situation their solution assumes.
  • 10–25 | Problem or solution? Compare “People need our app,” “Small shop owners struggle to reconcile sales records,” and “Small shop owners spend time comparing paper notes and mobile-payment messages at the end of the day.” Discuss which is most investigable and why.
  • 25–40 | Segment narrowing. Turn “all bicycle users” into a testable recruitment description. List who is not yet included and whether the group shares a relevant context.
  • 40–55 | Evidence language. Label six statements as evidence, interpretation, assumption, or hypothesis. Discuss how reported experience differs from independently observed behaviour.
  • 55–70 | Interview demonstration. Model a five-minute interview. First demonstrate a leading question, then restate it neutrally. Learners identify how each question changes the information produced.
  • 70–90 | Paired interview practice. Interview a partner about a recent difficult task. Do not discuss a business solution until the past experience is clear. Switch roles after eight minutes; record two direct observations or reports and one uncertainty.
  • 90–105 | Synthesis and pattern finding. Sort notes into “what happened,” “what the person said,” “what I infer,” and “what remains unknown.” Identify one repeated pattern and one difference. Role-play is practice, not market evidence.
  • 105–120 | Experiment design. Select the most important uncertainty and complete the experiment card. Ask what a positive or negative result would change and whether the method might mislead or pressure a participant.
  • 120–135 | Peer review. Check the segment, hypothesis, method, observable measure, threshold, time and cost, participant safeguards, and next decision. Revise the card.
  • 135–145 | Report-out. Present an experiment in one minute. Listen for a clear link between the uncertainty and the test.
  • 145–150 | Exit ticket. Complete: “The most important thing I do not know is ____. I will learn it by ____. The result will change my next step by ____.”

If only 120 minutes are available: Shorten the report-out and use one interview role-play round. Assign real customer conversations before the next class.

Learner worksheet 2: discovery interview guide

Opening script: “I am learning how people handle [task or situation]. I am not selling anything. May I ask about a recent experience? I will take notes without your name unless you agree otherwise.”

Questions:

  1. Tell me about the most recent time this happened.
  2. What were you trying to do?
  3. What happened from the beginning to the end?
  4. What did you do next?
  5. What was difficult, slow, expensive, risky, or frustrating?
  6. What alternatives did you consider or use?
  7. How often has this happened recently?
  8. What did it cost in money, time, effort, or missed opportunity?
  9. What happened when you did not solve it immediately?
  10. Who else was affected or involved in the decision?
  11. What have you already tried? What worked and what did not?
  12. What important detail have I not asked about?

Interviewer reminders: Ask one open question at a time. Ask about a recent specific event before opinions. Avoid explaining the solution while the participant describes the problem. Use neutral follow-ups; do not imply a preferred answer. Thank the participant and explain how notes will be used. Do not record or collect identity details without permission.

Knowledge check

  1. Which statement describes a customer problem rather than a solution? A. People need our app. B. We should build a mobile service. C. Weekday bicycle commuters in one area may struggle to transport a disabled bicycle to a workshop after a breakdown. D. Our service will be the best.
  2. Which question is most likely to produce useful discovery evidence? A. Would you buy this? B. Do you like our idea? C. Tell me about the last time you handled this situation. D. Would you use it if it were free?
  3. Why define an initial customer segment? A. To exclude every other customer forever. B. To make recruitment and comparison more focused. C. To avoid learning about alternatives. D. To prove the idea is successful.
  4. Which is an interpretation rather than direct evidence? A. The participant said they postponed the repair. B. A dated receipt records the repair charge. C. The founder thinks transport may be the most important barrier. D. The participant described walking the bicycle to a workshop.
  5. What is wrong with “Wouldn’t it be easier if a mechanic came to you?” during early discovery? A. It asks about a past event. B. It presents a solution and invites agreement. C. It is too open. D. It measures repair time.
  6. What should a useful experiment include? A. A hypothesis, method, measure, threshold, and next decision. B. A guarantee that the idea will work. C. A result chosen after the test. D. A large budget.
  7. What should the learner do when evidence conflicts with the business idea? A. Remove the conflicting note. B. Record it and decide what to revise or investigate. C. Say the whole market is different. D. Change the answer key.
  8. Which practice supports respectful discovery? A. Record participants without telling them. B. Promise a product launch to encourage participation. C. Explain the purpose, make participation voluntary, and collect only needed notes. D. Ask for personal details even when irrelevant.

Answer key: 1-C; 2-C; 3-B; 4-C; 5-B; 6-A; 7-B; 8-C.

Homework assignment: customer problem evidence and test plan

Conduct 5–8 short conversations or observations with people who meet a clear recruitment description. If access is limited, conduct fewer and explain the limitation; do not invent responses. Learners may work in pairs if both follow the same consent and note-taking practices.

Submit:

  1. The initial segment description and solution-neutral problem statement.
  2. An evidence log with at least five entries, or an explanation of the smaller sample.
  3. A short synthesis: repeated patterns, differences, evidence against the idea, and what remains unknown.
  4. One prioritized hypothesis and experiment card.
  5. A brief reflection: What did you change in your initial idea after listening?

Do not submit names or unnecessary identifying details. Do not make promises, charge money, or present a concept as an available service unless authorized and able to fulfil clear terms.

Assignment rubric: 20 points

Score each criterion from 0 to 4.

  • Customer segment and context: 0 = absent; 1 = very broad; 2 = some relevant context; 3 = specific and recruitable; 4 = specific, justified, and clear about who is excluded for now.
  • Problem statement: 0 = absent; 1 = solution claim; 2 = customer or context unclear; 3 = specific task, obstacle, and consequence; 4 = solution-neutral statement includes current response and distinguishes known from unknown.
  • Evidence and synthesis: 0 = invented or absent; 1 = source unclear; 2 = notes exist but interpretation is mixed; 3 = evidence is organized and patterns stated; 4 = evidence, interpretation, contradictions, and sample limits clearly separated.
  • Experiment design: 0 = absent; 1 = method unrelated to uncertainty; 2 = hypothesis or measure vague; 3 = clear method and observable measure; 4 = decision-linked test includes a pre-set threshold, feasible scope, and limits.
  • Ethics and reflection: 0 = safeguards absent; 1 = purpose or consent unclear; 2 = basic safeguards; 3 = voluntary participation and careful data handling; 4 = thoughtful safeguards and a specific learning-based revision.

Interpretation: 17–20 = ready to carry evidence into Lesson 3; 13–16 = promising draft with a gap to address; 9–12 = revise the segment, statement, or test; 0–8 = revisit the lesson tools with facilitator support. A score reflects the quality of the learning process, not whether the evidence supports the idea.

Facilitator adaptations and common difficulties

  • Large class: Groups of three take interviewer, participant, and observer roles. The observer marks leading questions and evidence language; rotate roles.
  • Small class: Use a full-group demonstration and pair practice. The facilitator can play a participant using a prepared scenario.
  • Online class: Use breakout pairs, a shared evidence sheet, and a clear time signal. Do not record calls unless all participants knowingly consent.
  • Limited customer access: Use relevant public records or a smaller, clearly described sample. Do not treat role-play as market evidence.
  • Low literacy or multilingual group: Read prompts aloud, permit responses in the learner's strongest language, use drawings or spoken notes, and pair learners for transcription with consent.
  • No business idea: Use the bicycle-repair example or a common, low-risk service problem. The lesson is about method, not idea originality.
  • Learners pitch too early: Pause and ask, “What did the person do the last time this happened?” Return to the recent-event story.
  • Customer group is too broad: Ask, “Who experiences this in the same context or for the same reason?” Require a recruitment description.
  • Compliments are treated as validation: Ask what action, purchase, workaround, or commitment has actually occurred.
  • Learners force a positive result: Reward honest uncertainty and useful next decisions. Negative evidence can prevent wasted effort.
  • Test has no threshold: Ask learners to write the decision rule before collecting more responses.

Transition to Lesson 3

Before Lesson 3, each learner should be able to explain which customer group is the current learning focus; what problem appears in that group's actual situation; what evidence supports the statement and where the sample is limited; what current alternatives people use; and which uncertainty matters most next.

Lesson 3 will use these inputs to draft a value proposition and set objectives. If evidence does not support the original problem, revise the problem statement before writing a value proposition.
