Module 3: Financial Planning and the Complete Business Plan

Lesson 3: Writing, Presenting, and Improving the Complete Business Plan

Level: Beginner | Suggested duration: 150 minutes

Lesson output: A coherent business plan, a short audience-specific presentation, and a revision log tied to evidence.

Lesson purpose and learning outcomes

This capstone brings the customer, market, business model, operating plan, team, risks, and financial projections together. A strong plan is tailored to its reader, distinguishes fact from assumption, and makes the next decision clear. The document is a living management tool, not a guarantee of success or a substitute for execution.

  1. Organize the evidence and decisions developed throughout the course into a complete plan.
  2. Write an executive summary that accurately reflects the full plan.
  3. Tailor detail to the reader while preserving honest assumptions and risks.
  4. Present the opportunity, evidence, model, funding need, and next step clearly.
  5. Use feedback and new results to revise the plan and track changes.

1. Assemble the plan

A traditional plan may include:

  1. Executive summary: business, problem, customer, offer, evidence, business model, team, key financials, and request or next step.
  2. Business and objectives: purpose, scope, stage, measurable priorities, and relevant legal or ownership information.
  3. Product or service: what is delivered, how it works, customer benefit, development status, and limitations.
  4. Market and competition: target segment, research methods, demand evidence, market estimate, competitors and substitutes, and source limitations.
  5. Marketing and sales: positioning, channel choices, customer journey, sales process, measures, and acquisition assumptions.
  6. Business model and operations: revenue, pricing, costs, delivery workflow, capacity, suppliers, quality, and contingencies.
  7. Team and governance: relevant skills, responsibilities, capability gaps, and decision ownership.
  8. Risks and milestones: key uncertainties, mitigations, tests, owners, decision dates, and responses to downside results.
  9. Financial plan and funding: startup costs, assumptions, projections, cash needs, financing source, use of funds, and repayment or ownership implications when applicable.
  10. Appendix: supporting research, quotes, permits, product evidence, and detailed calculations, with permission and confidentiality respected.

Not every reader needs every appendix. Keep the main narrative concise and place detailed evidence behind it. Draft the executive summary last so it matches the actual plan.

2. Make claims traceable

For each important claim, ask: Is it an observed fact, a sourced estimate, an assumption, a target, or a forecast? Record the source and date; define terms; disclose sample and geography limitations; and explain the decision implication. Make major assumptions visible in a separate table with confidence, impact, test, owner, and due date.

Use consistent numbers across the market estimate, sales plan, capacity, revenue forecast, cash-flow schedule, funding request, and milestones. Reconcile differences rather than hiding them. State both what evidence supports the case and what could invalidate it.

3. Tailor the plan to its audience

Founders and staff need responsibilities, milestones, and operating choices. A lender may focus on repayment capacity, cash flow, collateral, and downside resilience. An equity investor may focus on customer evidence, growth potential, differentiation, team capability, risk, and ownership terms. A partner may focus on shared benefits, responsibilities, and dependencies. These are typical interests, not promises of acceptance or universal standards.

Before sharing, identify the decision sought, detail required, confidential information involved, and the reader's format or submission requirements. Share only necessary personal, customer, or commercially sensitive data. Follow local law and any confidentiality agreement.

4. Present the plan clearly

A concise presentation can follow this sequence: customer and problem; evidence; offer and value proposition; market and alternatives; business and revenue model; route to customer; operations and team; financial outlook and funding need; risks and milestones; specific request or next step.

Use readable charts with units, periods, sources, and assumptions. Explain uncertainty rather than projecting certainty. Rehearse a short version and prepare for questions about customer behaviour, competitor response, acquisition cost, delivery capacity, cash runway, key assumptions, and downside plans.

5. Improve through review and updates

Ask reviewers to identify unclear claims, missing evidence, inconsistent assumptions, unaddressed risks, and the decision they still cannot make. Categorize feedback as fact-check, evidence gap, strategic disagreement, or presentation preference. Decide what to accept and record why; not every suggestion is correct or aligned with the business.

Update the plan after material evidence, cost changes, customer behaviour, regulation, team capacity, or financing terms change. Keep a version date and change log: what changed, evidence, decision, owner, and next review. A plan should evolve without rewriting history or relabeling forecasts as outcomes.

Capstone activity

  1. Assemble the course artifacts: customer problem and evidence, value proposition, research, model, operations/team plan, cost schedule, forecasts, and risks.
  2. Write a concise plan using the sections above; adapt length to the intended reader.
  3. Use a consistency check across target customer, demand, sales capacity, revenue, cost, cash, and requested funding.
  4. Prepare a three-minute presentation with one clear decision request.
  5. Receive peer feedback and add a revision log with at least three changes or explicitly justified non-changes.

Review checklist

  • Is the customer group specific and supported by evidence?
  • Does the problem describe customer circumstances rather than only the proposed product?
  • Are alternatives and competitors considered fairly?
  • Can the business deliver what it promises with the stated people, time, and resources?
  • Do the pricing and revenue assumptions connect to customer behaviour and costs?
  • Do financial statements use consistent assumptions and periods?
  • Are uncertainties, limitations, risks, and responses explicit?
  • Is the requested decision and next step clear?

Knowledge check

  1. When should the executive summary usually be drafted? A. Before any research. B. After the full plan, so it accurately summarizes it. C. Instead of writing a plan. D. Only after funding is received.
  2. What makes a financial plan credible? A. Optimistic numbers with no basis. B. Transparent assumptions linked consistently across sales, capacity, costs, and cash. C. A single revenue total. D. No downside case.
  3. How should feedback be used? A. Accept every opinion. B. Evaluate it against evidence, audience, and decisions, and record revisions. C. Ignore all criticism. D. Change only the design.
  4. What should trigger a plan update? A. Material new evidence or changed assumptions. B. A calendar date only. C. Nothing; plans are fixed. D. Only a new logo.

Answer key: 1-B; 2-B; 3-B; 4-A.
